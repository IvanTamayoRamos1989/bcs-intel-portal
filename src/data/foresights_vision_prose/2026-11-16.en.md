Baja California Sur does not hold together toward 2050 as a single coastal formula repeated from south to north. The reading that still stands — and that the portal’s demand–supply board summarises as constrained at state scale — is a polycentric system: Los Cabos as the tourism engine under water and power stress; East Cape in tension with protected areas; Todos Santos and El Pescadero as the Pacific amenity spine; La Paz as the service capital and water–energy node; Loreto as a tourism–conservation anchor; Comondú as an agricultural hub with relatively more room; and northern Mulegé as a thinly documented corridor, now with a remote generation tender. That sum of distinct roles remains the central hypothesis.

The water ceiling is not hypothetical. Of 39 aquifers with CONAGUA mean annual availability (2023) in the portal, 22 are in deficit and those balances sum to −105.1 hm³ a year, with notable deficits in Los Cabos, East Cape, Todos Santos and La Paz. In 2026 local coverage reports 25 illegal taps closed in Los Cabos, with estimated extraction above 300 l/s, and the governor places the second Cabo San Lucas desalination plant at 75 % completion (an additional 250 l/s; 655 l/s combined, to be finished in 2027). Desalination can shift the supply margin, but it does not turn a deficit aquifer into a surplus, nor does it cancel the ENCIG 2025 figure, cited in the same coverage, that only three in ten people in the state have a constant supply.

The coastal ceiling has become visible. A UABCS researcher attributes to El Niño a 15-to-30 cm rise in wave height and the collapse of barriers in Puerto San Carlos, and warns of extraordinary tides through the rest of 2026, with a possible — not certain — weakening in 2027. Added to that are floods in El Dátil and Los Cabos in early November, and the partial demolition of Hotel San Cristóbal in Punta Lobos, under Profepa review. We do not project a 2050 shoreline: the portal has no sea-level series long enough. There is a direction: the coast now conditions permits and beach vocations.

The Cabos engine is still the one that pushes hardest and hesitates most. SETUE recorded 70.0 % occupancy in Los Cabos in 2025, against 64 % in La Paz. The Hotel Association reported 78 % in the first two weeks of November 2026, four points above 2025 and the first stretch above the prior year since March. GAP, by contrast, reported 520.9 thousand passengers in Los Cabos in October (−4.6 % year-on-year; 5.87 million in January–October, −5.5 %), while La Paz has accumulated 1.19 million in the year (+8.7 %). Formal IMSS employment in BCS fell 1.1 % year-on-year in October (224,460 posts in September). These are not one series, so we do not extend them to 2030 or 2050: occupancy is up in a specific episode, with air demand and formal employment still below last year.

INEGI records constant-price GDP of 187,152 million pesos in 2024, 3.5 % above 2023, and FDI of 1,578 million dollars in 2025 (1,353 in 2024). Two annual cuts cannot support a 2050 projection. The Sea of Cortez Forum put forward, as a proposal and not as a portal figure, a 3.5 % annual pace for twenty years and a tripling of regional FDI; IMCO moved BCS from 8th to 5th between 2021 and 2026. The SIBCS peak appears at 689 MW in 2024 (for 2023 the portal records both 660 MW net and 675 MW), and the Oasis Mulegé tender — 72 MW solar, 20 MW / 80 MWh and a 20 MW electrolyser — is a geographic signal, still under bid.

After nine weeks of the feed (18 September to 15 November), the water-coastal stress scenario remains the most probable, around 42 %, but it gives up almost twenty points this week as Forum-driven economic intensity jumps and tourism and new power-supply signals improve. The baseline scenario rises to about 32 %; polycentric diversification, to about 26 %. None of these is a plan. Official series are short or single-cut, and 2030 and 2050 projections are marked insufficient: the vision is updated each week with what is measured, not with an invented arrival figure.
